Research

From the Large Hadron Collider to the deep Pacific

The group works on two experiments: ATLAS at CERN's Large Hadron Collider, and P‑ONE, a neutrino telescope in the Pacific Ocean.

At the Large Hadron Collider, CERN

ATLAS

One of the two general-purpose detectors at the LHC.

Photo: the ATLAS detector, by SimonWaldherr, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

SFU physicists study the properties of the Higgs boson, search for new long-lived particles and other signs of physics beyond the Standard Model, and work on detector calibration and track reconstruction.

How it works

  1. Protons collide

    Beams of protons from the Large Hadron Collider collide at the centre of ATLAS.

  2. Tracks are recorded

    Charged particles curve in a strong magnetic field, and the inner tracker records their paths. SFU helps build the new one.

  3. Energy is measured

    Calorimeters stop most particles and measure their energy. Muons pass straight through to the outer muon chambers.

For the High-Luminosity LHC upgrade, ATLAS is replacing its inner tracking detector. The SFU and TRIUMF team built the first tracker "petal" ready for installation and plans to make about 100 of the 400 petals being produced worldwide.

In the deep Pacific, off Vancouver Island

P‑ONE

The Pacific Ocean Neutrino Experiment: a planned neutrino telescope anchored to the deep seafloor.

Photo: the first P-ONE line being lowered for testing at Granville Island, June 2026. Simon Fraser University, from SFU News

SFU leads the project's science, with Professor Matthias Danninger as scientific spokesperson, and built calibration detectors and light emitters that measure the optical properties of seawater.

How it works

  1. A neutrino interacts

    Now and then a neutrino interacts in the seawater, producing charged particles such as a muon.

  2. Light is made

    Those particles give off faint Cherenkov light in the dark water.

  3. Modules detect it

    Optical modules on mooring lines about one kilometre long pick up the light.

Where things stand

  1. June 2026The first line is assembled at TRIUMF and wet-tested in Vancouver.
  2. NextDeployment about 2,700 metres down, connected to Ocean Networks Canada's NEPTUNE observatory.
  3. After thatA demonstrator of four or five lines.
