At the Large Hadron Collider, CERN

ATLAS

One of the two general-purpose detectors at the LHC.

Photo: the ATLAS detector, by SimonWaldherr, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

SFU physicists study the properties of the Higgs boson, search for new long-lived particles and other signs of physics beyond the Standard Model, and work on detector calibration and track reconstruction.

In 2012, ATLAS and its sister experiment CMS discovered the Higgs boson,2 the particle tied to the field that gives other fundamental particles their mass. It completed the Standard Model of particle physics, but the Standard Model still can't explain dark matter or why the universe is made of matter rather than antimatter. So ATLAS also hunts for new particles that could. ATLAS sees up to 1.7 billion proton collisions a second, and its trigger system keeps only the most promising: roughly one in a million.3

How it works

  1. Protons collide

    Beams of protons from the Large Hadron Collider collide at the centre of ATLAS.

  2. Tracks are recorded

    Charged particles curve in the 2-tesla field of a solenoid magnet,4 and the inner tracker records their paths. The curvature gives their momentum. SFU helps build the new tracker.

  3. Energy is measured

    Calorimeters stop almost all particles and measure their energy. Muons get through and are measured again in the muon chambers; neutrinos escape unseen, showing up as missing momentum.

The new ATLAS Inner Tracker (ITk)

For the High-Luminosity LHC, which is due to start around 2030 with about ten times as many collisions,5 ATLAS is replacing its whole inner detector with a new all-silicon tracker. The SFU and TRIUMF team was the first to produce a petal for installation (a wedge of silicon strip sensors for the tracker's end-caps) and plans to make about 100 of the 400 petals being produced worldwide.6

In the Antarctic ice, at the South Pole

IceCube

The IceCube Neutrino Observatory: a cubic kilometre of ice turned into a neutrino telescope.

Photo: the IceCube Lab at the South Pole, with the hose reel used for drilling. U.S. Geological Survey, public domain, via USGS

IceCube is P‑ONE's older sibling: the same idea, in ice instead of seawater. SFU's Professor Darren Grant was spokesperson of the IceCube Collaboration from 2017 to 2021,2 and also works on P‑ONE.

Neutrinos barely interact with matter, so they reach us straight from the most violent places in the universe, such as the surroundings of supermassive black holes, without being absorbed or deflected. IceCube was the first detector to see high-energy neutrinos from beyond our solar system, opening up neutrino astronomy.3 Its densely instrumented DeepCore region, and now the Upgrade, also make it a precision experiment on the neutrinos themselves.

How it works

  1. A neutrino interacts

    Now and then a neutrino interacts in the ice or the rock below, producing charged particles such as a muon. Many come up through the Earth, which blocks every other kind of particle.

  2. Light is made

    Those particles travel faster than light does in ice, so they give off faint blue Cherenkov light, which carries far through the deep, very clear ice.

  3. Modules detect it

    Each optical module records the light with a 25 cm photomultiplier tube,1 then digitizes and time-stamps it and sends it up the cable to the IceCube Lab on the surface.

Drilling into the South Pole

This time-lapse shows the drill site being moved into position at the South Pole for the IceCube Upgrade. Crews used a 5-megawatt hot-water drill to melt holes about 2.4 km deep into the ice, about three days per hole,7,8 then lowered the new strings of sensors into them before the water froze again.

Research areas

Students join an international collaboration of more than 450 people from 58 institutions in 14 countries,6 working in areas like these.

  • Neutrino astronomyFinding the sources of high-energy cosmic neutrinos, such as the active galaxy NGC 1068, alone or with telescopes that see light and gravitational waves.
  • Neutrino oscillationsMeasuring how neutrinos made in the atmosphere change type as they cross the Earth, with DeepCore and the new Upgrade.
  • Dark matter and new physicsSearching neutrino data for signs of dark matter and other physics beyond the Standard Model.
  • Reconstruction and calibrationWorking out each neutrino's direction and energy from the light in the sensors, often with machine learning, and mapping how light travels through the ice. More below.
600+new sensors and calibration devices

The IceCube Upgrade

In the 2025/26 South Pole season, five new strings went into the ice at the bottom centre of IceCube, far more densely instrumented than the originals. Their new sensors, including multi-photomultiplier modules, are two to three times as sensitive. The Upgrade will sharpen measurements of neutrino oscillations, and its calibration devices will map the ice so precisely that 15 years of archived data can be reanalyzed. It also tests sensor designs for IceCube-Gen2.7

In the deep Pacific, off Vancouver Island

P‑ONE

The Pacific Ocean Neutrino Experiment: a planned neutrino telescope anchored to the deep seafloor.

Photo: the first P-ONE line being lowered for testing at Granville Island, June 2026. Simon Fraser University, from SFU News

SFU's Professor Matthias Danninger is the scientific spokesperson of the P‑ONE collaboration.1,2 SFU also built precision calibration detectors and high-power light emitters that measure how light travels through the seawater.

P‑ONE uses the same technique as IceCube, but in the deep ocean. Its site in the northeast Pacific sees a different part of the sky to IceCube, so the two complement each other, and it can plug into Ocean Networks Canada's existing seafloor network for power and data.3 The ocean also brings its own challenges: the lines sway in the currents, and the water glows faintly from radioactive potassium and bioluminescent sea life.3

How it works

  1. A neutrino interacts

    Now and then a neutrino interacts in the seawater or the seafloor rock, producing charged particles such as a muon.

  2. Light is made

    Those particles travel faster than light does in water, so they give off faint blue Cherenkov light in a cone, at about 42° to their path.

  3. Modules detect it

    Optical modules spaced along mooring lines about one kilometre long pick up the light.2 When and where it arrives shows the particle's direction and energy.

Watch the first line go into the water

In June 2026, P‑ONE‑1 was lowered into the harbour at Granville Island in Vancouver for its last test before deployment. It spent more than 12 hours in cold seawater, and every system worked.6

TRIUMFwhere P‑ONE's lines are built

Building P‑ONE in Vancouver

P‑ONE's detector lines are put together in the cleanroom at TRIUMF, Canada's particle accelerator centre, where SFU group members work on hardware, integration and assembly. Parts arrive from Canada, the US and Europe, including SFU's calibration instruments. Each one is tested, then mounted on the kilometre-long backbone cable, and every power and optical-fibre connection is checked as the line comes together. P‑ONE‑1 was completed this way in April 2026.5 Next, the demonstrator's four lines will be assembled and tested at TRIUMF in 2027–2028.

SFU and TRIUMF

TRIUMF, Canada's particle accelerator centre in Vancouver, began in 1968 as the TRI-University Meson Facility of UBC, the University of Victoria and SFU. SFU is still one of its 21 member universities,1 and the group works with TRIUMF on two of its three experiments.

ATLAS

Canada's ATLAS Tier-1 data centre was at TRIUMF from 2007 to 2018, then moved to SFU's Burnaby campus in 2019, where TRIUMF runs it.2 A consortium of ten institutions led by SFU and TRIUMF operates it, and it supplies about 10% of ATLAS's Tier-1 computing worldwide.2,3 SFU and TRIUMF also build petals together for the new ATLAS tracker.4

P‑ONE

P‑ONE's detector lines are assembled and tested in TRIUMF's cleanroom: P‑ONE‑1 in 2026, and the demonstrator's lines in 2027–2028.5 TRIUMF also provides site infrastructure in Vancouver, and P‑ONE's data acquisition system is largely a TRIUMF deliverable.6
